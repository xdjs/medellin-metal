# Medellín Metal — research dossier

Research dates: 3–4 November 2026. Starting source: [Edward Effect’s reel](https://www.instagram.com/p/DbLcNMTSvUz/). Scope: Medellín’s ultrametal and the networks connecting Colombian music to international extreme metal, with Bogotá and the United States as additional paths. This is a sourced editorial prototype, not a comprehensive national history.

## 1. What the reel actually says

The starting reel was transcribed automatically using Apify. No manual listening verification of that transcription has been completed. Its English renderings “Reincarnation” and “Massacre” are normalized to the Colombian artists Reencarnación and Masacre in editorial copy.

The reel introduces an early-1980s Medellín metal scene, calls it Ultra Metal, names Reencarnación, Masacre and Kraken, describes correspondence between a Medellín drummer and a Mayhem founder, proposes that Medellín may have invented black metal before Norway, and points to an exhibition at Biblioteca Pública Piloto. These are distinct claims, requiring different evidence.

| Reel proposition | Editorial finding | Treatment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Medellín had an early extreme-metal scene | Supported by musician testimony, contemporary archival research and specialist writing | Core narrative |
| The named bands are interchangeable examples of ultrametal | Too compressed: Kraken’s heavy-metal route and Masacre’s death metal need separate treatment | Separate artist and listening paths |
| Tapes travelled between Colombia and Norway | Supported by retrospective firsthand accounts | Connect people and recordings; identify testimony as testimony |
| Colombia invented black metal before Norway | Not established as a sole-origin claim | Explain reported influence and parallel scenes, not a winner-takes-all invention story |
| Metal Medallo is open “right now” | Time-dependent and now outdated for the documented exhibition run | Historical exhibition entry, not a current visitor listing |

## 2. Medellín: music made by people, not a soundtrack imposed on a city

The most productive framing is agency: young musicians built instruments, organized rehearsals and circulated music. Violence and exclusion were real conditions, but reducing the music to cartel imagery reproduces the outside gaze the musicians were resisting. In the 2026 Loud Society documentary, participants describe the conditions of performance and the labor of making equipment. Their memories support an account of music as a social practice, while individual episodes should remain attributed recollections. [S2]

“Ultra” was a local language for intensity and difference. It is useful to treat ultrametal as a scene-specific aesthetic with porous borders, rather than to force every recording into later black/death/thrash categories. Invisible Oranges’ 2015 survey emphasizes both the recognizability of the sound and the differences among its bands. Listening should accompany those distinctions: a label on a graph is not a substitute for hearing an actual recording. [S3]

## 3. Parabellum and the archival problem

Parabellum is the necessary addition to the reel’s short list. Standard discographical accounts place its formation in 1983 and the releases Sacrilegio in 1987 and Mutación por radiación in 1988. Its later Tempus Mortis compilation made earlier material available to a new audience. Use those dates as release anchors, not proof that no earlier rehearsal or performance existed. English and Spanish reference summaries differ on the end of the band’s activity; this prototype does not silently choose one end date. [S4]

The 1985 Batalla de las Bandas is important because memories of it connect musical taste, access to recording, class and the right to occupy a public stage. Noisey’s documentary provides a primary oral-history route. Its machine transcript is error-prone, so it should guide a viewing and not supply exact quotations. Avoid turning a complex crowd event into a simple morality play with Parabellum and Kraken as opposing social stereotypes. [S5]

## 4. Reencarnación: a record as a meeting place

The 1988 album commonly identified as 888 Metal gives the experience a powerful listening entry. The Nuclear War Now! edition provides a label-hosted stream and historical notes. This is an edition of an earlier recording: the digital page’s release date is not automatically the recording’s original date. Víctor Raúl Jaramillo, known as Piolín, is a key voice in the scene’s retrospective accounts. His band’s experimentation also challenges the idea that technical limitation is a complete explanation for ultrametal. [S6, S2]

The site connects the artist to the record, Piolín, Medellín and the reissue label. A reissue edge means preservation and renewed circulation; it does not mean that the reissue label produced the 1988 sessions.

## 5. Kraken and Masacre: preserve the differences

Kraken widens the story beyond extreme-metal genealogy. Cartel Urbano places the band’s first appearance under that name at Teatro Lux in Manrique on 22 September 1984. Its heavy-metal identity, vocal approach and broader public reach give a valuable comparison with the raw early underground. The editorial should let a reader hear Kraken beside Reencarnación without pretending the bands had the same aesthetic. Elkin Ramírez is central to this route. [S7]

Masacre supplies a second route: a Medellín death-metal band whose international relationships are visible in actual release and production credits. The supplied reel’s early-1980s framing should not be applied indiscriminately to the formation of every named group. Documentary accounts place Masacre’s beginning in 1988. Its drummer Mauricio “Bull Metal” Montoya appears in oral histories as an important intermediary in international exchange. [S2, S5]

The later Total Death offers especially strong evidence. HateWorks explicitly credits Erik Rutan as producer, and the band’s old official album page distinguishes production by Rutan and Masacre from engineering by Freddy Torres. Those roles must remain separate; do not assign all recording work to the famous American name. [S8, S9]

## 6. What crossed the Atlantic?

The most defensible account is a network of exchange. Participants recall letters, records, tapes and cultural material moving through personal contacts. Bull Metal and Euronymous belong on the map as intermediaries. The documentary also contains a direct challenge to the sole-origin story: scenes developed in parallel and came into contact. [S2]

A widely repeated Euronymous quotation reaches later reporting through Emilio Cuesta’s account in the 2005 Tempus Mortis booklet. That is retrospective testimony, not a newly discovered contemporary interview. Multiple websites repeating it are not multiple independent witnesses. The original booklet and dated correspondence would strengthen the chain of custody. The prototype therefore labels the Parabellum/Reencarnación-to-Mayhem links “reported influence.” [S4, S10]

Three different things should never collapse into one arrow: an audible similarity (critical interpretation), an exchange of a recording (historical contact), and a musician’s statement of influence (testimony). Nor does prior chronology prove exclusive invention. A fuller scholarly treatment would compare Colombian developments with Venom, Bathory, Hellhammer/Celtic Frost and other international scenes using dated releases and direct statements.

## 7. Erik Rutan: the production connection

Masacre’s Total Death (2004) links the Medellín band to guitarist and producer Erik Rutan through the production credit in HateWorks’ catalog. Rutan had played in Morbid Angel and founded Hate Eternal in St. Petersburg, Florida, in 1997. Season of Mist’s artist biography identifies him on guitar and vocals. [S8, S19]

Rutan owns Mana Recording Studios in Florida. His 2021 interview with URM covers his time in Morbid Angel and the development of his studio practice. The interview provides a firsthand route into his career beyond this album. [S20]

Metal Blade’s Cannibal Corpse biography dates his first production work with the band to Kill (2006). Violence Unimagined (2021) was his first album as a full member; Chaos Horrific (2023) was his second and his sixth as producer. These are connections through Rutan’s career, not claims that those bands collaborated directly with Masacre. The explorer preserves those separate relationships. [S21]

## 8. The infrastructure deserves its own entries

Musicians are only part of the story. The archive should represent label editions, rehearsal spaces, luthiers, recording engineers, fanzines, films and festivals as navigable entities. The exhibition reporting identifies Luis Emilio’s rehearsal space, Fernando Correa “Nando” and handmade instruments, alongside tickets, photographs and other preserved objects. This makes a useful next phase of object-level research. Original photographs, named collectors, object dates and rights must be established before turning a press description into a catalog record. [S15]

Nuclear War Now!’s Reencarnación reissue and HateWorks’ Masacre release are already suitable label nodes because the relevant releases can be read and heard. Román González and Juan Diego Parra-Valencia are also important to the preservation story: documentaries and research shape what later audiences can know. [S6, S8, S15]

## 9. Beyond Medellín

A national title must not imply a complete national inventory. The first edition explicitly focuses on Medellín and its outward links. Bogotá is represented through Rock al Parque, which Idartes dates to 1995. The festival demonstrates public infrastructure and intergenerational circulation, a different mechanism from mail exchange. Its history is not exclusively metal. [S16]

Cali, Pereira, other regional scenes, women musicians, Indigenous perspectives and independent local production require further sourced chapters. That is an editorial expansion list, not permission to invent representative bands or make every city a derivative of Medellín. Future interviews should ask who provided equipment, who booked rooms, who recorded early sessions, how women participated, and what circulation looked like outside the best-documented bands.

## 10. Exhibition, memory and the danger of “now”

El Colombiano’s June 2026 reporting describes the Metal Medallo exhibition at Biblioteca Pública Piloto, an associated book and documentary project, and a focus on 1983–1993. It states that the exhibition would run until the last week of July. An October 2026 interview with researcher Juan Diego Parra-Valencia also refers to the exhibition retrospectively. Accordingly, the reel’s “right now” cannot become a current opening-hours promise. [S15, S17]

The exhibition suggests the right interaction model: an object opens into people, places, recordings and memories. The digital experience should retain attribution and uncertainty alongside immersion. A visitor can start with the story or a record, follow a person into another scene, and always reach the source supporting a connection.

## 11. Listening and image policy for the prototype

Use official artist or label streams when available. Reencarnación’s 888 Metal and Masacre’s Total Death have verified Bandcamp pages and album embed IDs extracted from those pages. Kraken’s Muere Libre has a discoverable YouTube upload, and Noisey’s Parabellum documentary supplies a viewing route. Persistent embeds allow navigation without restarting a selected recording; playback is initiated by the visitor and remains subject to provider availability, region and account restrictions.

No full songs are downloaded or rehosted. Album art and documentary thumbnails identify the linked source; they are not claimed as licensed publication assets. The local prototype uses them with source credits. Before any public launch, review image permissions and replace or license editorial photography as needed. Raw Apify output, credentials and full scraped source pages are not served by the local web server.

## Expanded documentary edition — 3 November 2026

This edition adds places, circulation and subsequent generations to the original reel-led account. It remains a history centered on Medellín, with selected Colombian and international routes. It is not an exhaustive national history. Connections are classified through their wording: membership, performance, release, production, correspondence or retrospective testimony. Shared nationality and sonic resemblance do not establish influence.

### Houses, venues and the work before a record

Juan Diego Parra’s December 2023 essay *Medellín con M de metal* describes the physical network behind the recordings. Luis Emilio Valencia, a musician in ArCo, converted a house near Santa Gema into a rehearsal space. Parra describes long daily sessions and Víctor Gaviria renting the space for bands recording music for *Rodrigo D. No Futuro*. The exhibition reporting supplies a second material connection: a homemade guitar built by Fernando “Nando” Correa and a drum kit with an earlier life in tropical music. These objects complicate an account organized only around genre names and famous international admirers.

Casa de Lucho Némesis, in Belén, hosted Saturday backyard concerts. Parra remembers an entrance price of 300 pesos and performances by Reencarnación, Masacre, Ekhymosis and others. This is attributed recollection, not an independently audited venue ledger. His account of the Carlos Vieco theater describes the suspension of rock concerts after violence against Kraken in 1987 and their return in 1990. Differences among audiences, venues and bands matter; the story should not flatten the city into a single uniformly violent scene.

Source: [Universo Centro, Juan Diego Parra, December 2023](https://universocentro.com.co/medellin-con-m-de-metal/). Photographs reproduced there include the Román González archive and Juan Fernando Ospina. The displayed rehearsal-house photograph depicts Oscar Osorio, not Luis Emilio Valencia. A caption explicitly preserves that distinction.

### Masacre in the 1990s: a record and a language

Fernanda Ibarra’s 2024 interview with Alex Oquendo gives a musician’s explanation for Masacre’s Spanish lyrics: the events the band described occurred in Spanish. The public story reproduces only his short phrase, “lo que pasaba en Colombia, pasaba en español,” and attributes it. The interview describes the 1991 European release of *Reqviem* through Osmose Productions and the resulting press, radio and audience reach. It also supplies a useful listening route chosen by the musician himself: “Brutales Masacres” from *Reqviem*. The artist’s Bandcamp dates the album to October 1, 1991.

Sources: [Switch interview, August 18, 2024](https://revistaswitch.com/masacre-35-anos-de-pasion-crudeza-y-brutalidad-entrevista/); [Masacre’s official Reqviem stream](https://masacremetaloficial.bandcamp.com/album/reqviem). This chapter fills the gap between the early tape network and the later Rutan collaboration. It does not treat access to Europe as the first moment the music acquired value.

### More than one Colombian route through Erik Rutan

In a 2015 Tough Riffs interview, Internal Suffering bassist Andrés García states that Rutan produced the band’s previous two albums. He also describes relocating to Madrid, where the singer and drummer lived, and working next with Stefano Morabito. That is a distinct career route from Medellín’s early ultrametal history. The current edition does not infer a shared local scene from a shared nationality or producer.

Sources: [García interview](https://toughriffs.weebly.com/interviews/interview-with-andres-garcia-internal-suffering); [Unique Leader’s official Awakening of the Rebel stream](https://uniqueleaderrecords.bandcamp.com/album/awakening-of-the-rebel). The latter lists November 27, 2009. This is shown as a digital-edition date rather than silently replacing an original-release chronology. Original pressing documentation is needed before expanding the album-level engineering credits. A fan comment on Bandcamp is not a label production credit.

### Later generations and women’s participation

Radiónica’s June 27, 2024 report documents Lilith, a Medellín rock band led by guitarist and songwriter Tatiana González. It discusses the collaborative *Sin miedo* project and Laura Azul becoming the singer. A 2011 Orbita Rock interview offers an earlier audiovisual entry point. Lilith is included as a later generation of the city’s rock circuit, not retrospectively inserted into the original ultrametal roster. This addition does not resolve the lack of primary interviews with women who organized, photographed, distributed, attended or performed in the 1980s scene.

Sources: [Radiónica](https://www.radionica.rocks/musica/artistas-colombianos/lilith-lanza-nueva-cancion-y-prepara-nuevo-disco); [Orbita Rock interview](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=c2DUBaQasE8).

### Remaining historical work

The original correspondence and *Tempus Mortis* booklet still need direct examination. The reel’s machine transcript needs checking against the audio. Women’s histories in the original period and histories outside Medellín need interviews and local archives, rather than extrapolation from this selection. A comprehensive account of Colombia would require separate treatment of Bogotá, Cali, Pereira and other scenes. No missing testimony has been replaced with invented quotations, dates or listening timestamps.

## Opening context: before Parabellum

The new prologue separates four questions: where this history happens; the political conditions surrounding young people; the rock scene that preceded ultrametal; and the circulation of recordings and friendship groups. It introduces Colombia, Antioquia and Medellín before naming a band.

The political framing follows the [Centro Nacional de Memoria Histórica report](https://centrodememoriahistorica.gov.co/micrositios/basta-ya-medellin/), especially its distinction between cartel terrorism, political violence and the wider armed conflict. The [MUROCK historical exhibition](https://murock.org/exposicion_virtual/historia-del-rock-en-medelllin-lado-a-1964-1990/) provides the Ancón flyer and the earlier recording circuit. [Parra’s March 2026 interview](https://www.elcolombiano.com/cultura/documental-metal-medellin-historia-batalla-bandas-MB34356695) explains how imported records and cassette copies circulated. [Hacemos Memoria’s Castilla walking history](https://hacemosmemoria.org/2019/10/30/barrio-castilla-punkero-memorias-galladas-punk-violencia-y-de-resistencia-caminata-carlos-david-bravo-escritor-baterista-libro-mala-hierba/) brings in neighborhood organization and collective listening.

Editorial distinction: these conditions help explain opportunities, constraints and subjects of expression. They do not establish violence as a sufficient cause of any particular musical sound. Images in the prologue are dated/captioned musical documents, not purported photographs of political events.

## Editorial revision — 4 November 2026

The main reading path now has eight chapters: listeners, first bands, rehearsal and concert houses, the sounds, exchange networks, Masacre's Spanish-language death metal, remembered participants, and archives. Erik Rutan, Internal Suffering and Rock al Parque remain accessible as three optional routes and in the full timeline. This concentrates the main story on Medellín without deleting documented international connections.

### Rehearsing and recording are different events

[Juan Diego Parra's retrospective account in Universo Centro](https://universocentro.com.co/medellin-con-m-de-metal/) describes Luis Emilio Valencia's house as an ensayadero used for soundtrack rehearsals. [VICE's 2015 soundtrack history](https://www.vice.com/es/article/rodrigo-d-la-historia-de-la-banda-sonora-del-no-futuro/) identifies Chava Bluss as the recording studio, with sessions in June 1988. The narrative and profile now make that distinction explicitly. The streaming release's 2016 date is an edition date, not the formation date of Pestes, Mutantex or Amén.

### The local network before the overseas connection

Parra recalls Bull Metal's room in Florida Nueva, cassette copying, boxes of letters, NecroMetal (made with Alex Oquendo), and La cortina de hierro on Radioactiva. These are attributed recollections, not newly inspected physical issues or station logs. New profiles connect the fanzine, radio program, musicians and bands. A three-place guide situates rehearsal near Santa Gema, backyard concerts in Belén, and exchange in Florida Nueva. It deliberately supplies no invented street coordinates or present-day venue claims.

Parra's account of the March 1985 Batalla de las Bandas and Piolín's recollection support a discussion of divisions in the rock audience. These recollections should not be flattened into a claim that all members of any band or audience shared one social class.

### People and soundtrack bands

[Julio César Guzmán's 2026 interview with Ramiro Meneses](https://juliocesarguzman.substack.com/p/rodrigo-d-desde-el-futuro-segunda) supplies firsthand recollections of Mutantex rehearsals, borrowed instruments, his lyric notebook, and bringing punk knowledge to the film team. Meneses names Rosa Colorado as the person who brought casting news to their friends. The new Rosa entry clearly attributes that account to Meneses; it does not invent a formal film credit or substitute his testimony for hers.

VICE's soundtrack feature supplies differentiated histories of Pestes, Mutantex and Amén, as well as the controversy over participating in a film about sicariato. The article contains chronology and song-origin claims that require caution: its Ekrion dates conflict internally, and its account of “Sin reacción” as a “My Way” adaptation conflicts with other accounts. Those disputed claims have not been imported into the story. The original soundtrack sleeve, session documentation and participants' testimony remain preferable for a definitive credits reconstruction.
